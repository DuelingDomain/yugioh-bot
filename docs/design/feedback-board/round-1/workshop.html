Feedback board: three approaches

Three ways to build the feedback section you asked for. They use the same requirements, seed ideas and sample players, so the differences come from the concept alone. Each approach shows its model, five key screens, how it covers each requirement, and what it would cost to build. The comparison and the open questions come last.

What you asked for

A feedback section inside the web app, run from the existing owner admin console. The alpha is small and invitation-only (41 members), so every approach favours one-tap actions and a short owner queue over volume features. The requirement codes below are reused in every approach and in the comparison.

Approach A

Next Turn

A feedback board is a game of turns between the players and the team, and the board should always show whose move it is.

Players move first: they Back, Pass or suggest, usually right after a duel, draft or tournament. The team answers by approving, planning, building and shipping, and each answer is posted where everyone can see it. The board borrows three shapes from the duel screen: the six-station track, face-down cards and a numbered chain.

Want to click through every screen? Open the full interactive mocks.

The model

A duel ends. A player makes a move: a Back, a Pass or an idea. The team answers it, and every answer is visible. The loop is short on purpose, because a board only stays alive while each move gets a reply.

Trade-offs

Strengths

  • Progress reads at a glance because the track, the face-down card and the chain are shapes players already know.
  • The loop closes. Backers hear when it ships and are asked if it works, so a vote leads to an answer.
  • Intake rides on real play, so ideas arrive as one-tap votes at the moment of feeling.

Risks

  • It is the largest surface of the three: eleven statuses, prompts in six places and a weekly log.
  • The board is only as live as the owner. Status moves and updates are all by hand.
  • For 41 members the machinery may be more than the volume needs.

Build size

L

A new section with five routes and several tables, prompt slots in the result screens, a seventh admin view with four panes, and a weekly Shipped log composer. It ships in three phases.

Assumes and asks

What it assumes

  • Team means the owner allowlist for now.
  • A player must finish one duel, draft or tournament match before voting or posting.
  • Statuses are set by hand. Nothing moves from GitHub or deploys in the first version.
  • The owner clears about four waiting ideas a day, mostly by keyboard.

Questions it raises

  • Who counts as Team: only you, or a new flag for other people who post updates?
  • Should every idea need your approval forever, or can trusted players skip the queue?
  • Are you comfortable showing the soft-reset numbers for Ranked in public?
  • How do players get into Testing: a hand-picked list, an Account setting or everyone?

Approach B

Set list

A feedback board is a card set in the making: players propose cards, the owner picks which ones make the next print, and the shipped set is the record.

Ideas are proposal cards with a name, an area, a pitch and Back and Pass counts. The owner approves them into an open pool, then pulls a few into the upcoming numbered set, which has a release window and a limited print run. Pulled cards move through four plain stages, and a released set becomes a permanent numbered list that players rate with “Working well?”.

The model

  1. PlayerPost a cardA name, an area and a short pitch. Only you and the owner see it while it waits.
  2. OwnerApproveThe card goes into the open pool. Team cards start there too.
  3. EveryoneBack or PassPool cards have no stage and no date. Votes tell the owner what to pull.
  4. OwnerPull into the next setThis is the move into development. The print run, 8 cards, is the cap.
  5. OwnerAdvance the stageSlotted, Proof, Playtest, Printing, Released. Four stages plus a waiting slot.
  6. PlayersField reportAfter release, people who used the feature answer “Working well?” yes or no.

If a card misses its set, the owner carries it into the next set or sends it back to the pool marked Held over. It keeps its votes. Nothing drops out silently.

Cadence and owner workload

The owner works in three rhythms. Daily: a one-minute approval queue. Once a set: one planning pass to pull cards and set the window. At close: one shipped line per released card. The weekly Shipped log chore goes away, because the set list is the log.

What progress means

A pool card has no progress, and says so. The first step is being pulled. After that the card has a stage, and the set has a fill level and a window. Players ask “is it in Set 01?” instead of “what station is it at?”.

Ideas that miss a set

A miss is a clear answer, not silence: Not in Set 01. The card is carried over or returns to the pool as Held over, with its votes. After two holds the owner pulls it or shelves it with a reason.

Trade-offs

Strengths

  • A player gets a plain answer to “will I get this?”: in Set 01, in the pool, or held over. A pool card needs no status theatre.
  • The owner works in batches: a one-minute daily queue, one planning pass and one close per set. The weekly Shipped log chore goes away.
  • Each release is an event with a number, a window and a permanent list. Field reports tie to named cards, so “how it’s working” has a place.

Risks

  • A window and a print run are a public deadline for a solo owner. A slipped set can read as a broken promise.
  • Most pool cards show no movement for weeks. Without a regular pool note, members may think nothing happens to unpulled ideas.
  • Small fixes and bug-like requests do not fit a set. They need a between-sets path, or the set list leaves out quick wins.

Build size

M

New: a sets table, set and stage on ideas, a field-report table, the pack prompt, and the Plan page. It has fewer states than a per-idea status track and needs no new realtime.

Assumes and asks

What it assumes

  • The owner is willing to work in sets of about four weeks and to show a release window, not a promise.
  • A print run of 8 matches what one person can ship in a set. It replaces the three Building slots.
  • Code still deploys from main as it does now. Stages and releases are set by hand, with no deploy automation.
  • The 12 real shipped lines are gathered into Set 00, with no votes linked.
  • All six Ranked parts start open in the pool. The owner pulls them one at a time, and the most-backed part, Ranked queue, is the first pull candidate.

Questions it raises

  • How long should a set be, and should players see a window or only the set number?
  • Where do quick fixes and bug fixes go: a between-sets line, or only in the next set?
  • Should an unfinished card carry to the next set by default, or return to the pool?
  • Is a hard cap of 8 cards the right way to say no, or should the owner choose a size per set?

Approach C

The Floor

A feedback board is a lobby: ideas, tables and shipped work, ordered by what is happening now.

The Floor borrows the duel lobby. Work in development sits at live tables with a seat for kaito, a step count and the time of the last move, and ideas arrive on a fast stream sorted by recent activity. Shipped work becomes a post-game card with a live “Working well?” split. Two clocks run it: the players’ clock (backs per hour) orders the stream, and the team’s clock (the last move) lights the tables.

The model

The players’ clock orders the floor

Every back and pass is stamped with its hour. An idea with 2 backs in the last hour, or 5 in a day, is in Rising now. Anything with a new reply or vote moves up the stream. Nothing is sorted by status.

The team’s clock lights the tables

Live: moved in the last 3 days Resting: quiet for longer Booked: not started

The lamp is a fact about the last move, never a date or a promise. Ticking a step stamps a move on its own, so keeping a table lit costs one tap. A resting table says so calmly and nobody is chased.

The Shelf holds everything with no activity in 2 days: older ideas, parked ideas with their reason, and shipped work. It is sorted by total backs and searchable. A new back or reply lifts an idea back onto the floor.

  1. The owner holds the door. A new post waits there. The author sees it marked “At the door”. Approve lets it onto the floor, and team posts skip the door.
  2. Back and Pass both count, and both feed momentum. The stream is a feed of recent activity, and the Shelf is the archive. There are no status columns.
  3. Opening a table seats kaito at an idea. From then on it shows the seat, the steps done, the last move and a lamp. It never shows a date or a percentage.
  4. A move costs one tap. Ticking a step stamps itself with the time. A note is optional. People who press Watch are told.
  5. Shipping closes the table. The shipped line becomes a post-game card. Players who have played since are asked “Working well?” and the answers show as counts.

Finding older ideas

The top bar searches the stream and the Shelf together. The Shelf sorts by total backs, latest activity or parked. If someone replies to a three-week-old idea, it rises again with that reply as the reason.

The owner is never on stage

Nothing is due. A step tick writes the move for you, a resting lamp is neutral, and the floor stays lively from player activity alone. The one chore is the door, oldest first, and it has a count.

A week with nobody building

The tables area shrinks to one line: “No tables open. Next in line: Readable text everywhere.” The floor and Rising now carry the page, and the shipped strip says what is true, such as “Nothing shipped this week yet”.

Decision: Ranked starts as the hottest thread. All six parts are Open, and a table says the team is at the idea, so Ranked sits at the top of Rising now as one thread with six parts. The console proposes the first table on its most-backed part, Ranked queue (NT-102, 14 back), and you open it when you are ready. Screen 4 lets you try that move.

Trade-offs

Strengths

  • It looks and acts like the app’s own lobby, so a player reads a lamp, a seat and a watch count without a legend.
  • The board stays alive without the owner. Player activity moves the floor, and a step tick is the whole update.
  • It is honest in a quiet week. A resting table and an empty shipped strip are plain facts, not gaps.

Risks

  • With 41 members, momentum is noisy. Three backs in an hour can look like a trend, so thresholds need tuning.
  • Recency can bury a good idea. Only the Shelf, search and the lift from a new back save it.
  • There is no single view of everything planned or building. A player must read tables and the Booked row, or filter the Shelf.

Build size

M

The data is a plain idea table plus vote timestamps bucketed by hour. The extra work is the momentum query, tables with steps and moves, follows and 30-second polling for the lamp and stream.

Assumes and asks

What it assumes

  • The owner visits the console every day or two, mostly for the door.
  • Votes keep a timestamp, so an hourly count is possible.
  • Players accept a public “Resting” label on a table with no recent move.
  • “Watching” means following. The count is people who follow. It does not show who is on the page.

Questions it raises

  • Is a 3-day window for a lit lamp right, and are you comfortable showing “Resting” after it?
  • Should the first table open on Ranked queue as soon as you confirm the rank facts, or wait?
  • Should “Working well?” go to anyone who has played since, or only to the players who backed it?
  • Does “table” for a thing the team builds clash with duel tables?

My read

A is the most complete and the easiest for a player to read, and B and C each fix one of its weaknesses. My recommendation is a blend: A's track as the backbone, with C's live tables in place of A's Building and Testing lists, so a step tick is the whole update. Keep B's set list in reserve for later, if you start shipping in planned batches. Question 1 below is where we decide.
